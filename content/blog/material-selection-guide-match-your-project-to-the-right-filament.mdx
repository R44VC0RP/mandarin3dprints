There is no strongest or safest filament for every job. A stiff display stand, a flexible bumper, and an outdoor clip need different properties. Material selection narrows the candidates; it does not guarantee that a particular printed geometry will work.

## Ask Five Questions First

1. **Where will the part live?** Record temperature, sunlight, moisture, and exposure duration. A parked car is not the same as a climate-controlled room.
2. **What loads will it see?** Distinguish stiffness, impact, repeated bending, wear, and sustained load. Note the consequence of failure.
3. **Which dimensions and surfaces matter?** Fit and finish can limit otherwise suitable materials.
4. **What will touch it?** Name chemicals and their concentrations. Treat food contact, skin contact, pressure, and electrical requirements as separate qualification questions.
5. **What can the process support?** Check printer temperatures, drying, enclosure, nozzle, build surface, and ventilation against the specific filament instructions.

## A Shortlist, Not a Ranking

| Application | Candidates to investigate | Main qualification question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indoor display or form prototype | PLA | Will heat or impact matter? |
| General functional prototype | PETG, tough PLA, ABS | Which grade meets the actual load and temperature? |
| Long outdoor exposure | ASA, suitable PETG grades | UV stability, temperature, and long-term load |
| Flexible bumper or grip | TPU | Hardness, geometry, abrasion, and recovery |
| Wear-resistant moving component | Specific nylon grade | Moisture conditioning, friction, and dimensions |
| Higher-temperature part | Documented ABS, ASA, PC, or nylon grade | Heat-deflection test conditions and service life |
| Direct food contact | Purpose-made compliant product or validated process | Finished-article suitability, not polymer name |

Use the [Prusa filament material guide](https://help.prusa3d.com/filament-material-guide) as a comparison starting point, then read the exact product's data sheet. A family-level table cannot capture every blend or print condition.

## PLA: Useful Stiffness and Easy Prototyping

PLA is often a practical choice for indoor models, organizers, and prototypes. It can be stiff and dimensionally useful without being suitable for repeated impact or hot environments. Heat-softening behavior depends on the formulation; ordinary PLA should not be assumed safe in a hot car.

"PLA+" is a marketing category, not one standardized material. Compare the manufacturer's actual properties. Annealed PLA may behave differently but can change dimensions during treatment. Low odor also does not mean printing produces no emissions.

## PETG: A Candidate for Tougher Functional Parts

PETG often offers useful toughness and good layer adhesion with less warping than ABS. It can be appropriate for clips, housings, and garden accessories, but has trade-offs in stiffness, stringing, and sustained-load deformation.

Do not use a glass-transition figure near 80 C as a blanket operating-temperature rating. A loaded part can deform below that temperature, and automotive interiors can be demanding. The [PETG guide](/blog/petg-explained-when-and-why-to-use-this-versatile-material) separates those limitations from print settings.

PETG is not automatically food-safe because related PET is used in bottles. Additives, printer contamination, surface cleaning, and intended temperature and exposure all matter. [Prusa's food-contact guidance](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/food-safe-fdm-printing_112313) discusses these problems. Neither a different nozzle nor a coating alone certifies the finished object.

## ABS and ASA: Heat and Weather Trade-offs

ABS can provide higher heat resistance than ordinary PLA, while ASA is often selected for improved weathering and UV resistance. Neither is universally more rigid or impact-resistant than every PETG formulation.

Both can need a controlled printing environment and appropriate emissions controls. ASA is not a workaround for inadequate ventilation. For a part near electronics or engines, check temperature, flame behavior, chemical exposure, and load independently; the material name does not establish suitability.

## TPU: Flexibility Is Only the Start

TPU comes in different hardnesses and formulations. Shore hardness describes resistance to indentation, not the flexibility of every finished shape. Wall thickness and internal structure strongly affect how a part bends or compresses.

A constrained filament path and a validated profile matter. Direct drive can help, especially with softer grades, but it is not an absolute requirement for every TPU; some Bowden setups handle compatible grades. Confirm the printer manufacturer's guidance.

A flexible print is not automatically a reliable gasket, pressure seal, or skin-contact product. Test compression, recovery, leakage, and chemical compatibility for the intended use.

## Nylon and Filled Materials: Read the Grade

Nylon can offer useful wear resistance and toughness, with moisture uptake and conditioning affecting both printing and finished dimensions. Carbon or glass fibers may improve stiffness and reduce warping while changing impact behavior and nozzle requirements. They do not make a part immune to creep or layer separation.

The [nylon guide](/blog/nylon-3d-printing-strong-parts-for-demanding-applications) explains why PA6, PA12, and reinforced grades cannot share one service-temperature rating.

## Validate Before Committing to a Batch

For an indoor prototype, start with a manageable material and test form and fit. For a functional part, print a representative joint or loaded section in the intended grade and orientation. Record settings, condition the sample appropriately, and evaluate the property the application actually needs.

If failure could injure someone, cause a leak, expose electrical components, or drop a load, basic trial-and-error is not sufficient qualification. Use an appropriately engineered part or process. For routine low-risk work, a small sample is still much cheaper than discovering an unsuitable material halfway through a batch.
