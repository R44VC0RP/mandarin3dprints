Ordering a custom 3D print for the first time can feel like a leap of faith. Understanding the steps helps you set realistic expectations and compare providers.

## The Upload and Quote Phase

A provider usually needs a 3D model in an accepted format, often STL, OBJ, or 3MF. Some offer automated quotes; others review files manually. A useful quote should identify:

- **Physical dimensions** of your part
- **Estimated print time** based on size and complexity
- **Material options** available for your design
- **Price** based on all of the above

Check the dimensions carefully. Files designed in inches but interpreted as millimeters can produce a much smaller part than intended. An automated quote is not necessarily a printability review.

### What Affects the Price?

Three main factors determine cost:

**Size.** A larger part generally uses more material and takes more time. Doubling every dimension increases the model's volume eightfold, but pricing also depends on walls, infill, supports, and the provider's minimum charges.

**Material choice.** PLA is the most affordable option. PETG costs slightly more due to its enhanced durability and heat resistance. Specialty materials like TPU (flexible) or high-temperature filaments carry higher price tags.

**Complexity and print time.** Intricate details, lots of overhangs requiring supports, and certain geometries extend print time. More time on the printer means higher cost.

## The Review Process

Ask whether a human printability review is included, when it occurs, and whether file repairs cost extra. Providers differ; do not assume an accepted upload means the design is ready.

### What a Review Should Cover

**Printability.** Is this design physically possible to print? Some geometries that look fine on screen won't work in the real world. Floating parts, impossible overhangs, and features smaller than the printer can resolve all get flagged.

**Wall thickness.** Thin sections can fail or be fragile. For common FDM settings, 1.2mm is a useful starting point, but suitable thickness depends on the material, nozzle, geometry, and load.

**Orientation optimization.** How a part sits on the print bed affects strength, surface quality, and print time. Sometimes rotating a model 90 degrees cuts print time in half while improving quality.

**Support requirements.** Identify where supports are needed, whether they can be removed, and which visible surfaces may be marked.

### Communication Before Printing

Agree on how design changes will be approved before production. Ask for specifics if a provider flags thin walls, unsupported geometry, or a fit problem.

A review can catch avoidable problems, but it is not a guarantee of strength or fit. Critical features may need a prototype and physical testing.

## The Printing Phase

Once your design clears review, it enters the print queue. Here's what happens during actual production.

### Print Preparation (Slicing)

Your 3D model gets converted into instructions the printer can follow—a process called slicing. The slicer software determines:

- **Layer height** (finer layers = smoother surface but longer print time)
- **Infill percentage** (how solid the inside is)
- **Print speed** (balanced against quality)
- **Support placement** (where needed for overhangs)
- **Temperature settings** (optimized for the specific material)

The printer, slicer profile, and material must match. Settings still require judgment, especially when strength and surface finish compete.

### During the Print

A typical print takes anywhere from 30 minutes to 20+ hours depending on size. During this time:

- The printer heats up and calibrates automatically
- Filament feeds through the heated nozzle
- The print head traces each layer's pattern
- Layer by layer, your part takes shape

Some printers include failure detection or camera monitoring. These features can help, but do not detect every failure or replace inspection.

### Real-World Print Times

To give you a sense of scale:

- **Small items** (keychains, cable clips, small brackets): 30 minutes to 2 hours
- **Medium items** (phone stands, enclosures, figurines): 2 to 8 hours
- **Large items** (full-size props, big functional parts): 8 to 24+ hours

Confirm the provider's usable build volume. Supports and brims need room too; oversized models may require splitting and assembly.

## Post-Processing and Quality Check

The print doesn't go straight from printer to shipping box. Several steps happen first.

### Support Removal

If the print needed supports, removal may involve snapping, cutting, or sanding them away. Confirm whether support removal is included in the quote.

Support contact points can leave witness marks. Orienting a model so supports attach to less visible surfaces can help, but some geometry makes marks unavoidable.

### Cleanup and Inspection

A useful inspection covers:

- **Dimensional accuracy** (does it match the expected size?)
- **Surface defects** (any failed spots, blobs, or stringing?)
- **Structural integrity** (any cracks or delamination?)
- **Overall quality** (does it meet standards?)

Ask how defects are defined and handled. Reprint and refund policies vary, and a design flaw may be treated differently from a manufacturing defect.

### Optional Finishing

Many providers distinguish basic cleanup from additional finishing. Possible extras include:

- Light sanding of visible surfaces
- Drilling or tapping holes
- Test-fitting multiple parts together

Agree on finishing work and its cost before production begins.

## Turnaround Time: What's Realistic?

Print time is only part of the schedule. Queue length, review, finishing, and delivery also matter.

### Confirm the Full Schedule

Ask for an estimate that includes:
- Review and queue time
- Print time (varies by size)
- Post-processing and quality check
- Packaging

There is no universal turnaround time. Confirm whether an estimate is a shipping date, a pickup date, or an arrival date.

### What Can Extend Timing

**Large prints.** A 20-hour print simply takes 20 hours. There's no rushing the physics.

**Multiple revisions.** Design changes and approvals add time before printing can start.

**Queue depth.** During busy periods, a short print can still have a long wait before production.

**Material availability.** Most common colors stay in stock, but unusual requests might require ordering filament.

### Rush Orders

If timing is critical, ask the provider whether rush work is available and what it costs. Moving a job forward in the queue does not remove its print, cooling, or finishing time.

## What You'll Receive

When your order arrives, here's what to expect from the finished product.

### Surface Characteristics

FDM prints have visible layer lines. This is inherent to the technology, not a defect. Finer layer heights (0.12mm) minimize this effect; standard layers (0.2mm) are more visible but print faster.

The top surface shows a slightly different texture than vertical walls. The bottom—where the part contacted the print bed—comes out smooth and flat.

### Dimensional Accuracy

Ask the provider what tolerances it can achieve for the specific geometry, material, and process. A general +/-0.2-0.3mm estimate is not a guarantee. Use the [fit-measurement guide](/blog/how-to-measure-your-model-for-perfect-fit-every-time) and agree on a sample where fit is critical.

### Material Properties

Your part will have the properties of the material you chose:

- **PLA:** Stiff, good surface finish, softens above 60°C
- **PETG:** More flexible, impact-resistant, handles higher temps

A printed part can contain infill voids and direction-dependent layer bonds. Do not assume it has the properties of a solid injection-molded specimen of the same polymer. Use the [material selection guide](/blog/material-selection-guide-match-your-project-to-the-right-filament) to compare the exact grade and conditions.

### Packaging

Fragile features need protection during shipping. Ask how delicate parts will be packed, and photograph damage before discarding packaging.

## After You Receive Your Order

Got your print? Great. A few things to know about living with your new part.

### Break-In and Testing

Check dimensions, fit, and finish before putting a functional part into service. If something is wrong, document it and notify the provider within its stated reporting window. Do not load a visibly damaged part.

### Storage and Care

PLA and PETG are stable at room temperature. Keep them out of direct sunlight and away from heat sources (don't leave PLA parts in a hot car). Clean with mild soap and water if needed.

### Iterating on Designs

First print not perfect? That's common and expected in the design process. Many customers go through 2-3 revisions to dial in dimensions, tolerances, or features. Each iteration gets you closer to exactly what you need.

## Choosing a Provider

Prepare a specification using the [print-service handoff checklist](/blog/preparing-your-3d-model-for-printing): model revision, dimensions, material, quantity, critical tolerances, and deadline. Use it to compare quotes on the same basis.

The [local printing guide](/#local-printing) can help you explore alternatives to owning a printer. Confirm each provider's current capabilities and terms directly.
